Peptide purity vs net peptide content

Two numbers that sound alike and measure different things, and where the measured quantity on a Renvara report fits.

Shop the catalog (opens in a new tab)Open the COA library

By Renvara Labs. Published 2026-10-10. Sources are listed at the end. For laboratory research use only.

The short version

Chromatographic purity is the main peak’s share of all detected peaks. Net peptide content is the share of the dry powder that is actually peptide by weight. A sample can be very pure by chromatography and still contain water and counterions that the detector does not see, so the two numbers answer different questions and should not be swapped.

Chromatographic purity

Reversed-phase HPLC or UPLC separates the components of a sample as they pass through a column. A UV detector records peaks, and purity is the area of the main peak expressed as a percentage of the area of all peaks. The method sees what absorbs light at the detection wavelength. Water and salts do not, so they do not appear in the calculation. An impurity that elutes together with the main peak can also be missed.

Read the full section: Chromatographic purity

Net peptide content

Net peptide content describes how much of the weighed material is peptide. The remainder is typically water, which many peptides pick up because they are hygroscopic, and counterions. After preparative HPLC, peptides are usually delivered as trifluoroacetate (TFA) salts, and the counterion adds mass. Amino acid analysis and elemental (CHN) analysis are the usual ways to determine it. A peptide that is 100% pure by chromatography can still have a net peptide content below 100%.

Where “measured quantity” fits

Renvara’s public reports list a purity percentage and a measured quantity in milligrams for each component, next to the labeled strength. For example, lot DPS-7659536 lists MOTS-c at 99.66% purity and 10.11 mg measured against a 10 mg label. The library listing does not explain how the quantity was derived, so read the original PDF.

Show the list: Where “measured quantity” fits

This is why the two columns matter together. Purity tells you how clean the main peak is. The measured quantity tells you how much of the target the laboratory reports finding in the sample it tested. Neither replaces the other, and neither describes a lot other than the one named on the report.

Questions that separate the two

  • Does the report give a purity percentage, a quantity, or both?
  • Does it say how the quantity was determined?
  • If a counterion is mentioned, is the quantity stated for the peptide itself or for the salt?
  • Does the quantity sit close to the labeled strength, and does the report state a tolerance?
