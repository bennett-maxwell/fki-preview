How to Hire a Handyman in Utah: A Homeowner Checklist

Educational guidance for Wasatch Front homeowners from Su Casa Handyman. Practical, safety-minded, and written to help you decide — not to scare you into a sale.

Hiring help for your home is a trust decision. You are inviting someone into your space, your schedule, and your budget. Use this checklist to evaluate any handyman — including us — with clear eyes.

Checklist 1. Define the outcome2. Proof & protection 3. Scope clarity4. Communication 5. Red flagsFAQ

1. Define the outcome (not just the broken part)

Write the result you want: “Door closes quietly and seals against dust,” not only “door is broken.” Photos from multiple angles, approximate ages of materials, and access constraints (pets, kids, HOA, parking) save everyone time.

2. Proof & protection

  • Ask about licensing and insurance appropriate to the work.
  • Look for real reviews with specifics (communication, cleanup, follow-through).
  • Prefer a reachable business identity: real phone, real service area, real owner or manager name.
  • For {BRAND}: call {PHONE}, review our values, and see reviews.

3. Scope clarity beats the cheapest number

Cheap bids often omit prep, protection, disposal, or return visits. Ask:

  • What is included / excluded?
  • Who supplies materials?
  • What happens if we open a wall and find a surprise?
  • How are change orders communicated?

4. Communication standards

Pros confirm arrival windows, explain delays, and walk through completed work. {BRAND} core values put customer-first listening, honesty, and accountability at the center — ask any company how they handle mistakes. The answer matters more than a perfect sales pitch.

5. Red flags

  • Pressure to pay large cash-only sums without a clear scope
  • Unwillingness to discuss insurance or references
  • Guarantees that sound absolute for conditions nobody has inspected
  • Disappearing communication after the first message

Hiring FAQ

Should I get three bids every time?

For larger scopes, comparing approaches (not only prices) is smart. For small urgent safety items, speed and trust may outweigh a full bid tournament.

Is the lowest bid usually fine?

Only if scope, prep, and protection match the higher bids. Apples-to-oranges pricing is how projects sour.

What should I prepare before the visit?

Clear access to the work area, decide pet plans, gather product manuals if relevant, and list all punch items so sequencing is efficient.

Questions to ask on the first call

  • What similar jobs have you completed in the last 90 days?
  • Who will be in my home, and how do you screen crew members?
  • How do you protect floors and furnishings?
  • What does your warranty or make-right process look like?
  • When do you take payment, and what methods do you accept?
  • How do you handle hidden conditions once work starts?

Write answers down. Patterns matter more than charm. Companies that get impatient with scope questions often get impatient mid-project too.

Photo package that gets better bids

Send a wide shot of the room or elevation, a mid shot of the assembly, and a close-up of the failure. Include a coin or tape measure for scale when cracks or gaps are the issue. Note ceiling height, exterior access, and whether furniture must be moved. Mention HOA painting rules or product brand preferences up front so bids reflect reality.

Comparing bids fairly

Create a one-page scorecard: scope completeness, prep/protection, materials path, timeline, communication plan, and total. A mid-priced bid with excellent scope clarity often outperforms a low bid that forces change orders. Beware allowances that are unrealistically low — they are not savings; they are deferred arguments.

During the job

Agree on a daily tidy standard for multi-day work. Confirm how leftover materials are handled. If you will not be home, set decision rules for small discoveries under a dollar threshold you choose. Great handyman relationships feel collaborative, not adversarial.

After completion, walk the work in good light. Operate doors, run water, flip breakers if relevant, and photograph final conditions for your home records. Leave a specific review if the experience was strong — detailed reviews help neighbors more than star counts alone.

Utah-specific practicalities

Confirm business legitimacy with the same care you would for any home-access trade. Keep records of scopes and invoices for warranty and home-sale disclosures. In winter, ask how weather delays are communicated. In summer, ask how heat-sensitive products (certain adhesives, exterior coatings) are scheduled so cure windows are realistic along the Wasatch Front.

Neighborhood access differs between downtown Salt Lake blocks, foothill driveways, and South Valley HOA gates. Share codes and parking rules early. If you live near canyon winds, mention exterior ladder exposure so staging plans are honest.
