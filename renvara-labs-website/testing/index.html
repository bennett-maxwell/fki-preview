How Renvara Labs tests: the seven named methods

A plain-language guide to each test in the stated seven-point program, what it can and cannot show, and which results the public COAs list today.

Shop the catalogOpen the COA library

The stated seven-point program

In short

Renvara Labs states that every peptide it offers goes through a seven-point analytical and microbiological testing program performed in Utah: HPLC/UPLC, mass spectrometry, identity or sequence, LAL/BET endotoxin, elemental impurities, residual solvents and sterility. That is the company's own description. The six public lot reports listed today show UPLC/MS purity, identity and quantitation.

A purity result is useful, but it cannot answer every quality question. Each test below looks at a different attribute of the material, which is why one number never tells the whole story. Each section says what the test measures, why it is a separate check, how to read it on a report and which published standard it is usually read against.

Descriptions of Renvara’s program are from renvaralabs.com/product-testing, captured 2026-10-10. Method explanations are general laboratory background with the sources listed at the end.

Each test, explained

Test 1 of 7

HPLC / UPLC: purity and related impurities

Renvara describes it as: High-performance or ultra-performance liquid chromatography evaluates the sample's chromatographic purity profile and detects related impurities.

What it measures. Liquid chromatography separates the components of a sample as it passes through a column. A detector, commonly UV absorbance, records each component as a peak. Purity is usually reported as the main peak's area as a percentage of the total peak area. UPLC applies the same principle with smaller particles and higher pressure for sharper separations.

Why it is a separate check. It shows how much of the signal comes from the intended peptide and how much from related impurities such as truncated or modified sequences. It cannot see water, salts or other species that do not absorb at the detection wavelength, so a purity percentage is not the share of the powder that is peptide.

How to read it on a report. Find the method and detection wavelength, the chromatogram, the retention time of the main peak and the reported area percent. Then compare the product name and lot number on the report with your vial.

Reference standard: USP general chapter <621> Chromatography. Source (opens in a new tab)

Test 2 of 7

Mass spectrometry: molecular mass and identity

Renvara describes it as: Mass spectrometry compares the observed molecular mass with the expected molecular mass to support confirmation of compound identity.

What it measures. A mass spectrometer measures the mass-to-charge ratio of ions made from the sample. For peptides, several charge states of the same molecule often appear, and software converts them into one observed molecular mass.

Why it is a separate check. Comparing the observed mass with the expected mass supports confirmation of the compound's identity. A match does not separate two peptides that share a mass but differ in sequence, which is why sequence-level evidence is a separate test.

How to read it on a report. Look for the expected mass, the observed mass, the stated tolerance and the ionization method named in the report.

Reference standard: USP general chapter <736> Mass Spectrometry. Source (opens in a new tab)

Test 3 of 7

Identity / sequence: intended peptide confirmation

Renvara describes it as: Method-appropriate identity or sequence analysis supports confirmation that the tested material corresponds to the intended peptide. The level of sequence confirmation depends on the method used.

What it measures. Identity analysis asks whether the material is the intended peptide. The depth depends on the method: fragmentation mass spectrometry (MS/MS) can read part or all of a sequence, while amino acid analysis checks composition rather than order.

Why it is a separate check. Mass alone leaves room for sequence isomers. Sequence-level evidence narrows that gap, and the level of confirmation depends on the method used.

How to read it on a report. Check which method the report names, whether it states sequence coverage, and whether the result is a statement such as 'conforms' or a data table you can inspect.

Reference standard: none single; the method named on the report sets what is confirmed.

Test 4 of 7

LAL / BET endotoxin: bacterial endotoxins

Renvara describes it as: Limulus amebocyte lysate or bacterial endotoxins testing screens the sample for bacterial endotoxins.

What it measures. Bacterial endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. The Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) test mixes the sample with a lysate reagent that reacts to endotoxin. Gel-clot, turbidimetric and chromogenic readouts are the three technique families.

Why it is a separate check. Endotoxin can carry through from manufacturing steps and water, and it can interfere with cell-based in vitro assays even when the peptide itself is pure by chromatography.

How to read it on a report. Results are given in endotoxin units (EU) per milligram or per milliliter, with the limit and the method stated. A purity report does not contain this result.

Reference standard: USP general chapter <85> Bacterial Endotoxins Test. Source (opens in a new tab)

Test 5 of 7

Elemental impurities: heavy-metal screening

Renvara describes it as: Elemental analysis checks for contaminants such as lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury.

What it measures. Elemental analysis, usually by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) or optical emission (ICP-OES), measures trace metals. Renvara's description names lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury, the four Class 1 elements in the USP and ICH framework.

Why it is a separate check. Metals can enter from catalysts, reagents, equipment or containers, and they can affect assay chemistry. A purity percentage does not report them.

How to read it on a report. Look for element-by-element results with units (often micrograms per gram) beside stated limits. For a copper-containing compound such as GHK-Cu, copper is part of the molecule, so read any copper result with that in mind.

Reference standard: USP general chapters <232> and <233> and ICH Q3D. Source (opens in a new tab)

Test 6 of 7

Residual solvents: manufacturing-solvent screening

Renvara describes it as: Residual-solvent analysis checks for solvents that may remain following the manufacturing process.

What it measures. Residual-solvent analysis, commonly headspace gas chromatography, detects organic solvents that can remain after synthesis and purification.

Why it is a separate check. ICH Q3C groups solvents into three classes: those to be avoided, those to be limited and those with low toxic potential. Leftover solvent can also change how a material behaves in an assay.

How to read it on a report. Check which solvents were screened, the result for each, the limit and the units (usually parts per million).

Reference standard: USP general chapter <467> Residual Solvents and ICH Q3C. Source (opens in a new tab)

Test 7 of 7

Sterility testing: viable microbial contamination

Renvara describes it as: Sterility testing evaluates the sample for viable microbial contamination under the conditions of the test.

What it measures. Sterility testing looks for viable bacteria and fungi in the sample under the conditions of the test. Typical approaches are membrane filtration or direct inoculation into growth media, followed by incubation and observation. USP <71> calls for incubation of not less than 14 days.

Why it is a separate check. Contaminating microbes can overgrow or confound cell-culture work. A sterility result applies only to the sample that was tested.

How to read it on a report. Look for the method, the media, the incubation period and a statement such as 'no growth observed'. Do not infer a sterility result from a purity report.

Reference standard: USP general chapter <71> Sterility Tests. Source (opens in a new tab)

What the public COAs show today

In short

The COA library lists six reports, one per product, all analyzed on 2026-09-16 by Horizon Analytical and listed as UPLC/MS purity, identity and quantitation. No endotoxin, sterility, elemental-impurity or residual-solvent report is published for any of the six lots. This site does not claim that any lot passed all seven tests.

Renvara’s own Terms say the same thing in general form: a certificate describes the sample, methods and results identified in that report, and a purity result does not establish sterility or endotoxin status. Use the report that matches your lot, and ask support when the documentation you need is not listed.
