Franchise ROI Analysis — How to Calculate Whether a Franchise Is Actually Worth It

Everyone who buys a franchise thinks they've done the math. Most haven't. They've looked at gross revenue figures from a polished presentation and assumed the gap between revenue and investment represents profit. It rarely does.

A real franchise ROI analysis starts with the full cost picture and ends with a defensible projection of what you'll actually take home — under conservative, base, and optimistic scenarios.

The Total Investment Number Is Not Your Capital Outlay

The franchise disclosure document shows a "total investment" range. This range almost always understates the real capital requirement for the first 12-18 months.

What's Typically Excluded

- Working capital — most FDDs list a working capital figure that covers 3 months. Many franchisees need 6-9 months to reach break-even.
- Living expenses — if you're replacing a salary, you need personal income during the ramp period
- Marketing spend above the ad fund — local marketing requirements often exceed the FDD estimate in competitive markets
- Owner's time — if you're operating the business personally, your time has an opportunity cost

True capital requirement = FDD total investment + 6 months personal living expenses + contingency reserve (10-15% of total)

Building the ROI Model

Step 1: Establish Your Revenue Scenarios

Pull Item 19 data and identify:
- Conservative scenario: 25th percentile annual revenue for mature units
- Base scenario: median annual revenue for mature units
- Optimistic scenario: 75th percentile annual revenue for mature units

Step 2: Calculate Gross Profit

Apply the franchise's typical gross margin to each revenue scenario. For service businesses, gross margins are typically 50-65%. For food/beverage, 30-45%. For retail, 35-50%.

Step 3: Subtract Fixed Costs

Fixed monthly costs include:
- Royalties (typically 4-8% of gross revenue)
- Marketing fund contributions (1-4% of gross revenue)
- Rent or lease payments
- Labor (if fixed staff)
- Software, insurance, and compliance costs

Step 4: Calculate Owner's Discretionary Earnings

What remains after fixed costs is owner's discretionary earnings (ODE) — the pool available for debt service, owner compensation, and reinvestment. This is the number that determines whether the investment is viable.

Interpreting the Results

The Break-Even Test

At conservative (25th percentile) revenue, does ODE cover:
1. Your debt service (if you financed the investment)?
2. A minimum acceptable owner compensation?

If yes: the investment is defensible. If no: you're betting on above-median execution in a new business.

The 5-Year IRR Test

Calculate the internal rate of return on your total capital invested over 5 years, assuming:
- Year 1-2 at conservative revenue
- Year 3-5 at median revenue
- Terminal value = 2-3x ODE (typical franchise resale multiple)

A well-structured franchise investment should deliver 20-35% IRR over 5 years when modeled at median unit economics.

The Opportunity Cost Test

What would your capital return if invested in a diversified index fund? (Approximately 7-10% annually.) Your franchise must justify the additional risk, illiquidity, and operational involvement with meaningfully higher returns.

Common ROI Calculation Mistakes

Using gross revenue as a proxy for profit. Gross revenue tells you almost nothing about what you'll earn. Find the net or EBITDA figures. Ignoring the ramp period. Most franchise models take 12-24 months to reach mature unit performance. Your ROI model must account for below-target revenue during ramp. Not modeling the exit. Franchises are assets. A business generating $150,000 ODE annually typically sells for $300,000-$450,000. This terminal value is a significant component of total ROI.
